Where does the
decision wait?

Known responsibilities do not necessarily mean a decision moves smoothly. Examine the sequence of approvals, reviews and escalations.

  1. RequestWaiting for reviewEntry to the decision
  2. ReviewReturning to the same checkRepeated review
  3. DecisionWaiting for the final decisionEscalation dependence
  4. CompletionReopening an agreed decisionDecision reopening
Look more closely at this example

This lens asks about waiting and routing within the decision process. A repeated review may be required, optional or duplicated; its purpose and consequences need to be checked before changing it.

Reported delay alone does not prove that an approval is unnecessary. Retain required controls and examine the decision records alongside the reported conditions.

A controlled measurement process

The questions are structured. The scoring is repeatable. The explanation is separate.

Monderman does not ask an AI model to invent a score. Within the same instrument and scorer version, the same structured answers produce the same score. When enabled, AI helps explain a completed Diagnostic or Synthesis result; it does not calculate or change the score. Generated interpretation can contain errors and requires review. The current Diagnostic uses guided questions; interview mode is not available.

Structured inputA defined diagnostic records answers for a stated role, scope, sector, and run length.
Versioned scoreScoring rules are applied before explanation, and the version is saved with the result.
Bounded interpretationThe report explains the completed result. Time and labor-cost estimates remain separate, directional scenarios built from disclosed inputs.See the scoring architecture →

Beyond sentiment

Reported experience shows the strain. Structural evidence shows where to look.

How people describe work matters. By itself, that description cannot show which ownership, approval, handoff, or process condition may be involved. Monderman asks about those conditions directly.

Reported experience

What people encounter.

Engagement, morale, confidence, and experience measures record how people report the organization feeling or functioning.

Structured conditions

What the operating system asks of them.

Questions about ownership, authority, handoffs, routing, process load, and performance discipline examine how work is organized in practice.

These are different forms of evidence. Monderman preserves the distinction instead of treating one as proof of the other.

Evidence limits and planning assumptions

Reported conditions are not proven causes. Role-specific questions remain distinct. This overview shows one complete action from the moderate option; the full report retains all options, prerequisites and risks.

Illustrative example. 30 measured people over 28 days. 12 planning months. Spending uses current or documented planned baselines. All three benefit categories have been assessed.

Low case
$54,720 lower spending; $36,660 avoided future spending; $184,861 retained capacity.
Central case
$77,220 lower spending; $54,953 avoided future spending; $260,404 retained capacity.
High case
$101,400 lower spending; $71,280 avoided future spending; $331,941 retained capacity.

Low case: net existing-spending effect after cash costs -$13,880; net spending effect versus current and planned baselines $22,780. Combined value after all costs, low case: $183,641.

Combined value after all costs, central case: $310,977. Net existing-spending effect after cash costs: $13,620. Net spending effect versus current and planned baselines: $68,573. This is not a measured bank-balance change.

Low, central and high are input cases, not probability bounds. Retained capacity can fall as more hours fund spending benefits. Gross capacity is spread evenly across planning months; the full report reconciles its allocation. Costs include internal staff time. Read the full report and assumptions.
